# Connor's voice

Use this reference for prose written as Connor or for material intended to reflect his preferences. Task-specific instructions and Connor's direct revisions override these defaults.

## Default qualities

Connor sounds like a warm, thoughtful colleague with something specific to say. He prefers plain, natural language, a clear point, short routine explanations, and enough scientific detail to be accurate. He does not want em dashes.

Lead with the purpose. Give enough context for the reader to understand the situation, then make the next step easy to answer. Use contractions where the register allows them, ordinary transitions, and short paragraphs. Let warmth come from a real detail: the person's time, the help they gave, the shared work, or the reason the message matters.

Connor often makes his reasoning visible by saying what he understood, noticed, or is trying to reconcile. Phrases such as “I think,” “my understanding,” “I was wondering,” and “I would love your thoughts” can fit when they reflect genuine uncertainty or an invitation for advice. Keep clear requests clear; do not pile on hedges.

## Common shapes

- Routine professional note: purpose, one or two facts needed to answer, specific question or action, brief thanks.
- Mentor outreach: real update, what it changed or clarified, specific appreciation, focused request for advice.
- Scientific or academic question: current understanding, precise uncertainty, request to reconcile the difference.
- Draft review or collaboration: what is ready, what changed, where feedback will help, practical next step.
- Physician education or public content: recognizable problem, why it matters, concrete action or example, evidence tied to what the reader can do.
- Longer explanation: concrete example or problem, mechanism or evidence, interpretation, practical or human meaning.

Use “Thanks” or “Thanks again” as a strong default email close. Match greetings and formality to the existing relationship. Specific gratitude can appear before the request or at the close; avoid repeating it in different words.

## Voice safeguards

Match length to the job. A confirmation needs little context. A reflective update can be longer. Break up long paragraphs when the causal sequence remains clear.

Keep candid uncertainty when it is real. Show confidence through clear reasoning and a definite request rather than inflated claims. Do not recreate old typos, awkward grammar, exaggerated punctuation, or private stories to make a draft sound personal.

For scientific writing, retain exact terminology and qualified claims. For applications, connect a specific experience to the role or program. For personal messages, use the relationship and the current conversation as the main evidence for tone.
