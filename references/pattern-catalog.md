# Pattern catalog

Use this catalog for substantial rewrites, critiques, and explicit requests to humanize prose. It adapts broadly useful observations from Wikipedia's field guide, Anthropic's prompting guidance, Microsoft guidance, corpus research, and editing resources. The catalog is descriptive. A single item does not establish AI authorship, and many are valid when the context calls for them.

## 1. Content and reasoning

### Inflated importance

Watch for routine facts framed as pivotal, profound, enduring, transformative, a testament, a milestone, part of a broader movement, or evidence of a sweeping legacy. The same problem appears when every detail is tied to culture, identity, innovation, or the future without support.

Revise by stating the fact, the supported consequence, and the scale. If the significance is an interpretation, identify whose interpretation it is and provide evidence.

### Canned notability and recognition

Watch for lists of media outlets, awards, coverage, or affiliations used as a substitute for explaining what happened and why it matters. “Awards and recognition,” “legacy and impact,” and other paired headings can become automatic containers rather than useful organization.

Revise by naming the actual award, coverage, decision, or documented effect. Keep a heading only when it helps the reader find distinct material.

### Superficial analysis

Watch for a factual clause followed by an unsupported “-ing” tail that tells the reader what the fact supposedly highlights, underscores, ensures, reflects, symbolizes, fosters, contributes to, or aligns with. Also watch for interpretations that merely restate the sentence in more abstract language.

Revise by deleting the tail, showing the mechanism, quantifying the consequence, or attributing the interpretation to a source that actually makes it.

### Promotional tone

Watch for travel-brochure and marketing language: vibrant, breathtaking, renowned, groundbreaking, world-class, nestled, boasts, rich heritage, diverse array, seamless, revolutionary, game-changing, unparalleled, commitment to excellence, and similar praise without evidence.

Revise by naming the feature, result, comparison, or evidence. In persuasive writing, make the case with concrete benefits and limits rather than generic superlatives.

### Vague actors, opinions, and associations

Watch for “experts say,” “critics argue,” “observers note,” “some believe,” “sources indicate,” or claims that something is “associated with,” “connected to,” or “linked to” another subject when a precise relationship should be available.

Revise by naming the actor, source, role, date, action, and degree of certainty. If the source does not support the claim, remove it.

### Generic balance and future outlook

Watch for outline-like endings about challenges, opportunities, continued evolution, promising prospects, future directions, and the need to adapt. These paragraphs often add no new information and end on automatic optimism.

Revise by identifying a real unresolved question, constraint, decision, or next step. End when the point is complete.

### Uniform coverage

Watch for every topic receiving the same amount of explanation, the same neutral tone, or the same pattern of definition, benefits, challenges, and conclusion. This can hide weak prioritization.

Revise around what the reader needs to know or decide. Give more space to the consequential or uncertain parts.

## 2. Words and sentence constructions

### Dense clusters of model-favored vocabulary

Words worth a contextual second look include: additionally, align with, bolster, crucial, deep dive, delve, emphasize, enduring, enhance, facilitate, foster, garner, highlight, interplay, intricate, key, landscape, leverage, meticulous, navigate, pivotal, realm, robust, showcase, significant, tapestry, testament, underscore, utilize, valuable, and vibrant.

Do not ban these words. Examine density and fit. Keep a precise technical use, such as “robust” in a statistical context. Elsewhere, prefer the concrete noun, verb, number, or condition. Often the best edit is deletion rather than synonym substitution.

### Throat-clearing and reader management

Watch for “It is important to note,” “It is worth mentioning,” “In today's fast-paced world,” “When it comes to,” “Needless to say,” “At the end of the day,” and repeated reminders that something is crucial to understand.

Revise by starting with the actual claim. Keep a warning or framing sentence only when it changes how the reader should interpret or act on the information.

### Heavy transition scaffolding

Watch for repeated sentence openings with additionally, furthermore, moreover, consequently, notably, overall, in summary, or in conclusion. Also watch for “that being said” and other connectors where the logical relationship is already obvious.

Revise by deleting the transition, using a plain conjunction, or reordering the ideas. Keep a formal transition when it carries real logical work.

### Avoidance of simple forms

Watch for inflated substitutes for “is,” “are,” “has,” or “uses”: serves as, stands as, represents, marks, boasts, features, offers, holds the distinction of, and functions as. The issue is frequency and needless ceremony.

Revise with the simple verb unless the stronger verb adds meaning.

### Stock contrasts and negative parallelism

Watch for repeated constructions such as “not only X, but Y,” “not just X; it is Y,” “it is not X, but Y,” “rather than X, Y,” and “more than just.” They often manufacture a misconception the reader did not have.

Revise by stating the positive claim directly. Keep the contrast when the competing interpretation is real and relevant.

### Rule-of-three padding

Watch for repeated triads of adjectives, nouns, clauses, examples, or benefits, especially when the third item is vague or redundant. Also watch for perfectly parallel lists whose shape seems to control the content.

Revise by keeping the items the idea requires. Two, four, or one may be right. Break the parallel structure when differences among the items matter.

### Excessive hedging

Watch for stacked qualifiers such as may potentially, could possibly, generally tends to, arguably might, or appears to suggest. Also watch for unsupported certainty at the opposite extreme.

Revise to the narrowest supported claim. State the condition or uncertainty once.

### Nominalizations and abstract containers

Watch for strings of abstract nouns and generic containers such as landscape, ecosystem, space, realm, framework, journey, and paradigm when the sentence can name the actual field, market, process, decision, or change.

Revise with a concrete subject and active verb. Preserve a term when it is the recognized name of the concept.

### Repetition and elegant variation

Watch for restating a point in the next sentence, paraphrasing every quotation, repeating the prompt, or swapping synonyms merely to avoid using the same precise term. Artificial variation can make references ambiguous.

Revise by removing duplicate thought. Repeat the correct technical noun when clarity benefits.

## 3. Structure, rhythm, and formatting

### Template-shaped organization

Watch for automatic introductions, previews, numbered sections, summary sections, and offers of further help when the medium does not need them. Common residue includes “Here is…,” “In this section, we will…,” “This guide will explore…,” “I hope this helps,” and “Let me know if you would like a more detailed breakdown.”

Revise into the requested deliverable. Remove model-to-user chatter and placeholder instructions before delivery.

### Uniform rhythm

Watch for sentences of similar length and syntax, repeated subject-verb openings, one-sentence paragraphs in a mechanical sequence, or paragraphs that each end with an abstract takeaway.

Revise by joining or splitting where the ideas call for it. Put important information early. Read aloud and correct the places that sound staged or breathless. Do not add randomness for its own sake.

### Excessive visual scaffolding

Watch for title case on every heading, bold lead-ins on every bullet, too many headings, lists made from ordinary sentences, decorative emojis, unnecessary tables, and ornamental divider lines.

Revise with the smallest structure that helps the reader scan. Use prose for connected reasoning and lists for genuinely parallel items.

### Punctuation tics

Watch for repeated em dashes, colon-led slogans, parenthetical asides, scare quotes, and inconsistent straight or curly quotation marks. These marks are normal in human writing; frequency, inconsistency, and genre fit determine whether they need editing.

For Connor, replace em dashes with commas, periods, parentheses, or a rewritten sentence because he has directly requested no em dashes.

### Register drift

Watch for sudden shifts between academic, corporate, therapeutic, promotional, and casual language; changes in regional spelling; or unexplained changes in fluency. Multiple authors and quoted material can legitimately create variation.

Revise toward the intended audience while retaining purposeful shifts.

## 4. Evidence and artifact integrity

### Unsupported or fabricated sourcing

Check every quotation, title, author, date, statistic, DOI, ISBN, URL, and page number. A polished citation can still be false, unrelated, or too weak for the claim. Do not invent a citation to make prose look complete.

### Source overclaiming

Check whether verbs such as proves, demonstrates, confirms, highlights, and acknowledges match what the source actually says. Separate a source's finding from the writer's interpretation.

### Markup and template leakage

Remove accidental Markdown, citation placeholders, search-result tokens, model-specific reference markers, empty brackets, prompt fragments, template variables, and code fences that do not belong in the destination format. Verify that links and references still resolve after editing.

### Abrupt or synthetic endings

Check for cut-off sentences, a sudden “Conclusion,” repeated closing summaries, generic calls to action, and moralizing final lines. End with the natural consequence, request, decision, or last necessary fact.

## 5. Critique rubric

Rank findings by their effect on the reader:

1. Accuracy and evidence: fabricated, unsupported, misleading, or overclaimed material.
2. Purpose and reasoning: missing point, weak logic, generic analysis, or buried decision.
3. Specificity and voice: vague actors, abstract language, stock phrasing, or poor audience fit.
4. Rhythm and structure: repetition, mechanical symmetry, over-formatting, or canned endings.
5. Surface wording: isolated watchlist terms, punctuation, and minor polish.

For each material finding, identify the excerpt, explain the reader-facing problem, and propose an edit. Group repeated instances under one pattern. Avoid turning the critique into a count of suspect words.
